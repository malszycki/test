# General
- Splicing, intron composition (not length) and importantly most core spliceosome components are common across all eukaryotes, implying that splicing evolved very early and was present in very similar form to today in that last common ancestor to all eukaryotes [@Collins2005ComplexSpliceosomalOrganizationAncestralExtant]
	- U5 binding proteins Prp8 and Brr2 are found throughout basal eukaryotes [@Collins2005ComplexSpliceosomalOrganizationAncestralExtant]
	- yeast U2 snRNP proteins belonging to SF3a and SF3b complexes are also conserved across all basal eukaryotes
	- DExD/H helicases, cyclophyllin and [[Splicing#SR proteins|SR proteins]] are not found in all eukaryotes suggesting, that they evolved later [@Collins2005ComplexSpliceosomalOrganizationAncestralExtant]. But RS motif sequences most likely were present in the eukaryotic ancestor
- Introns in highly expressed genes are much shorter, than introns in low expressed genes (14x shorter in humans, 2x shorter in *C. elegans*) [@Castillo-Davis2002SelectionShortIntronsHighlyExpressed]

- The last common ancestor of eukaryotes had numerous introns, and positions of many of these introns are conserved across many eukaryotic lineages. These introns were probably short, but since they evolved from G2introns initially must have had 400-800 or up to 2kb, but got degraded to ~160nt as in most eukaryotes to date. These ancestral eukaryotes had already SR proteins and hnRNPs and very likely alternative splicing (but most likely intron retention only) [@Irimia2014OriginSpliceosomalIntronsAlternativeSplicing]
	- Ast was always wrong (assuming that Alternative splicing is higher eukaryotes)
- Intron loss is much more common than intron gain or intron expansion. Wyjątek are animals, which have introns expanding in size due to the transposons [@Irimia2014OriginSpliceosomalIntronsAlternativeSplicing]
	- The rare occurrences of intron gain are concentrated in several episodes of rapid intron gain [@Irimia2014OriginSpliceosomalIntronsAlternativeSplicing]

- Nonsense Mediated Decay and likely ubiquitin based protein degradation most likely evolved together with the spliceosome and were present in ancestral eukaryote [@Irimia2014OriginSpliceosomalIntronsAlternativeSplicing].

# SR proteins and alternative [[Splicing]]
![[Splicing#SR Proteins]]

- [[Splicing#SR Proteins|SR proteins]] and hnRNPs as well as SREs (Splicing Regulator Elements: ESEs and ESSs) are present in all eukaryotic kingdoms, so most likely evolved early together with the spliceosome. But the alternative splicing is not present in all eukaryotes (none in *S. cerevisiae*) and the number of SRs and hnRNPs as well as the amount of alternative splicing increases with organismal complexity [@Busch2012EvolutionSRProteinHnRNPSplicing].
	- actually other studies show, that maybe all Yeast introns are involved in alternative splicing and can regulate the ribosome activity [@Irimia2014OriginSpliceosomalIntronsAlternativeSplicing; @Parenteau2011IntronsRibosomalProteinGenesRegulate; @Pleiss2007RapidTranscriptspecificChangesSplicingResponse].

- most likely SRs and hnRNPs were present in ancestral eukaryote, where they helped with canonical splice site recognition. But their presence reduced selective pressure allowing splice site degeneration and evolution of weak splice sites, that **enabled alternative splicing** (splice sites recognized only if SR present) [@Busch2012EvolutionSRProteinHnRNPSplicing].
- SR proteins and hnRNPs most likely evolve due to duplication and accumulation of mutations in the paralogues. All domains of these proteins show signs of accumulation of mutations, so it is not likely, that they evolved through domain shuffling [@Busch2012EvolutionSRProteinHnRNPSplicing]

- Humans have 12 SR proteins (6 paralogous families). SRSF10/12 is human specific. There is also a bunch of SR proteins specific to *A. thaliana*, and they lack proper homologues in human [@Busch2012EvolutionSRProteinHnRNPSplicing]
- SR proteins expanded in plants most likely due to genome duplication and relaxed evolution of SR paralogues [@Busch2012EvolutionSRProteinHnRNPSplicing]
	- More recent analysis shows, that outside of A. thaliana the number of SR proteins in plants is more "normal" (around 10) [@Guo2025DiversificationEvolutionSerineArgininerichSplicing], but still there are families, which completely lack homologues in human

- Humans have the most hnRNPs among all kingdims of life tested in [@Busch2012EvolutionSRProteinHnRNPSplicing]. It is likely due to their important function of suppressing pseudoexons.


# Why genes in pieces?
- presence of an intron increases expression (in mammals) [@Shabalina2010DistinctPatternsExpressionEvolutionIntronless; @Brinster1988IntronsIncreaseTranscriptionalEfficiencyTransgenic], but at the same time highly expressed genes are under selection for short introns [@Castillo-Davis2002SelectionShortIntronsHighlyExpressed]

- Introns protect genome from R-loops, but differentiating the RNA from it's DNA template (co-transcriptional splicing required) [@Bonnet2017IntronsProtectEukaryoticGenomesTranscriptionAssociated]

- in Yeast, the few remaining introns, seem to be required for starvation stress response. They are more common in ribosomal genes, which are repressed (through intron retention) during starvation to block protein translation. Also introns were shown to act in the same direction by contributing to stress response through TORC pathway [Box1 in @Rogalska2022RegulationPremRNASplicingRolesPhysiology] [@Parenteau2019IntronsAreMediatorsCellResponse; @Morgan2019ExcisedLinearIntronsRegulateGrowth]
	

# Most likely scenario of intron (and spliceosome) evolution
from [@Irimia2014OriginSpliceosomalIntronsAlternativeSplicing]
1. alpha-proteobacterium with plenty of G2introns invaded archeabacterial-like protoeukaryote and got symbiotized
2. G2introns invaded host genome
3. G2introns in the host genome proliferated wildly (no one knows why) 
4. High availability of Intron proteins, which could work in trans relieved selection on the G2introns, and they started degrading
5. Decaying G2introns split into snRNPs and their intron proteins evolved eventually into the core spliceosome
6. Lsm/Sm proteins present in bacteria (no splicing function there) were co-opted for the splicing
7. U12 (minor) splicing was most likely more ancestral, since it has more constrained sequence features and evolutionarily U12 was observed to evolve into U2, but not vice versa.
8. Expansion of spliceosome auxiliary factors relieved the sequence constrains from splice sites and they started decaying into the very degenerated splice sites we know today.
