# General
- Bacterial self splicing introns.  They are present in most bacteria, but also in **chloroplasts and mitochondria** of **fungi and plants**, but are rare in archea and  completely lacking from nuclear genome of eukaryotes [@Lambowitz2015MobileBacterialGroupIIIntrons]
-  They **share catalytic mechanism with the [[Splicing|spliceosomal introns]]**. Branch site attacks 5'exon exposing its OH, which then reacts with 3' exon excising the group II intron as a lariat (ibid)
- They always encode **[[Retrotransposons|Reverse Transcriptase]]**, which is involved in both stabilization of the pre-splicing #ribozyme structure and DNA-Primed-Reverse-Transcription into it's DNA localization. There are two types of Intron Encoded Protein (IEP) (ibid):
	- RT + EN ( [[Endonuclease]]s )
	- RT without EN
- IEP is translated from the gene locus (unspliced intron), binds to it's RNA and stabilizes the self-splicing RNA fold, then travels with the lariat to it's DNA of origin.
- They preferentially **re-integrate into the same gene**, but have very little off-target insertion rate as well (ibid).
- In *T. elongatus* the introns evolved system similar to **LINE and SINE [[Retrotransposons]]**, where some Group II introns encode RT, which can process introns lacking their own RTs (shorter and more efficient) ( ibid)
	- they also use the high temperature because they hybridize with melted DNA.
- When Group II introns are integrated into nuclear yeast genes they are not spliced in the nucleus, but they are spliced in the cytoplasm and the mRNA encoding the intron is degraded. Lariat is not degraded though [@Lambowitz2015MobileBacterialGroupIIIntrons] 

- Mutations in Group II introns can disrupt their splicing and make them genotoxic. To some extent it is rescued by splicing in *trans*, by other gropu II introns and their ribo/enzymes [@Martin2006IntronsOriginNucleusCytosolCompartmentalization]

# Group II introns in the evolution of splicing
- Eukaryotic [[Splicing]] most likely evolved from bacterial group II introns (G2introns) [@Lambowitz2015MobileBacterialGroupIIIntrons]. 
- They are RNA ribozymes that encode a reverse transcriptase and can splice themselves out of the RNA and insert to host DNA through [[Retrotransposons]]-like DNA-primed reverse transcription (ibid). 
- They are present in most bacteria, but also in chloroplasts and mitochondria of fungi and plants, but are rare in archea and  completely lacking from nuclear genome of eukaryotes (ibid)
- They share catalytic mechanism with the spliceosomal introns (lariat pathway). Branch site attacks 5'exon, exposing its OH, which then reacts with 3' exon excising the group II intron as a lariat [@Lambowitz2015MobileBacterialGroupIIIntrons]. 
- U2, U5 and U6, which are present in the catalytic spliceosome particle, have their equivalent in the G2introns.
- snRNAs can catalyze both spliceosomal reactions *in-vitro* without any spliceosomal proteins, showing that they have ribosyme activities just like G2intron RNAs [@Irimia2014OriginSpliceosomalIntronsAlternativeSplicing].

- G2introns probably got fragmented during evolution and appropriated into spliceosomal snRNAs, notably U2, U5 and U6 [@Lambowitz2015MobileBacterialGroupIIIntrons] 
- In most bacteria there is only one or a few G2introns, but in some strains they escaped host defences and proliferated a lot. A similar thing must have happened in early eukaryotes (ibid)

- Spliceosomal introns require much lower Mg^2+ concentration, probably through the activity of protein co-factors. Injection of Mg^2+ to the *X. laevis* oocyte and some other cells enables efficient Group II intron activity [@Lambowitz2015MobileBacterialGroupIIIntrons]. 
	- It is possible, that the lower Mg^2+ concentration is what prevents function of Group II and enabled spliceosome evolution. 
- [[Splicing#PRP8|PRP8]], which binds U2, U5 and U6 has structural similarities to Group II intron Reverse Transcriptase [@Galej2013CrystalStructurePrp8RevealsActive]. Although differences in the endonuclease domain and presence of RNase-H -like domain in Prp8 and lack of it in G2introns may suggest, that Prp8 evolved from Reverse Transcriptase of some non-LTR retrotransposon [@Lambowitz2015MobileBacterialGroupIIIntrons] 

In 2006 Martin and Koonin proposed, that Nuclear membrane evolved to separate intron-containing nascent RNAs from processed mRNAs, thereby preventing translation of RNAs containing introns [@Martin2006IntronsOriginNucleusCytosolCompartmentalization].  
- In 90s there was a lot of studies on *Giardia intestinalis*, which was suspected not to have mitochondria. It was later proven wrong: these protists lost their mitochondria due to reductive evolution [@Tovar2003MitochondrialRemnantOrganellesGiardiaFunction]

- consensus is that spliceosomal introns and the snRNAs resulted from the import of mitochondrial genes, containing G2introns, to the nucleus and got split, degraded and incorporated into the genome, most likely within the first common ancestors of all eukaryotes (as all eukaryotes have mitochondria and introns) [@Martin2006IntronsOriginNucleusCytosolCompartmentalization]

- Mutations in G2introns can disrupt their splicing and make them genotoxic. To some extent it is rescued by splicing in *trans*, by other gropu II introns and their ribo/enzymes, but is less efficient [@Martin2006IntronsOriginNucleusCytosolCompartmentalization]
- This decay of G2introns would impose selective pressure to form a spliceosome = *trans*-acting efficient splicing machinery

- G2introns in eukaryotic organelles are often degenerated, inactive and unable to self-splice. For splicing they often require host encoded proteins, or intron-encoded-proteins in trans. This may reflect the way G2introns became spliceosomal introns in the host nuclear genome [@Irimia2014OriginSpliceosomalIntronsAlternativeSplicing]
